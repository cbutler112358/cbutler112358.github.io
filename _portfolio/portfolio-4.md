Pyrethroids are the primary insecticides sprayed for mosquitoes in suburban North Carolina. These insecticides work by disrupting normal nerve cell function, causing a knockdown effect. Resistance to this effect is known as "knockdown resistance", or <i>kdr</i> for short. 

Researchers first genetically identified <i>kdr</i> mutations in Raleigh <i>Ae. albopictus</i> populations in 2020---only a dozen of 412 sampled mosquitoes possessed <i>kdr</i> mutations (<a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35640258/" target="_blank"  rel="noopener noreferrer">Abernathy <i>et al</i>., 2022</a>). Then, in 2023, I collected nearly 1,000 tiger mosquitoes from across Raleigh to assess the prevalence of <i>kdr</i> and examine the association between homeowners' socioeconomic status and insecticide resistance in <i>Ae. albopictus</i>. We found that <i>kdr</i> frequency was significantly associated with higher property values and increased dramatically, rising explosively from 4.3% in the 2020 study to 52.1% in only three years. 

These findings led to the publication of our results in <i>Royal Society Open Science</i>. We also published a companion paper in <i>Parasites & Vectors</i> that extended the mosquito sampling timeline to 2016-2024. You can find these papers
